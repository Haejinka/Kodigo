CHAPTER 1
INTRODUCTION
In the Philippine market, many local businesses still rely on localized Point of Sale (POS) systems that do not communicate effectively with a central database, leading to delayed or inconsistent records. This study documents the development and evaluation of Kodigo, a cloud-based inventory and financial management application for small and medium enterprises. The current system uses connected, server-validated operations and scoped records to support sales, inventory, and reporting; selected POS and inventory changes can be queued in the browser during connectivity interruptions. The system is designed to reduce manual reconciliation and provide a consistent operational view for participating stores.

Background of the Study 	
The global business landscape has undergone a significant paradigm shift, evolving from manual, paper-based ledger systems to localized digital tools, and effectively toward integrated cloud ecosystems. Internationally, the adoption of cloud technology has become a cornerstone for Small and Medium Enterprises (SMEs) aiming to scale, as these platforms allow for real-time data synchronization across disparate geographical locations. However, while global markets have largely transitioned to these automated environments, a substantial portion of the SME sector in developing regions remains tethered to fragmented legacy systems. According to Nevalga (2022), while Philippine MSMEs recognize the relative advantage of cloud adoption, they often face significant barriers related to implementation costs and a lack of government-supported infrastructure.
In the Philippine market, this digital divide is not merely a lack of hardware but a structural issue regarding connectivity. The current operational standard for many local businesses relies on localized Point of Sale (POS) terminals that function in isolation. Unicommerce (2026) identifies this reliance on manual documentation and inconsistent tracking as a primary threat to local brands, creating data silos where inventory movement at the branch level does not reflect in the financial master data. Consequently, business owners are forced to depend on delayed manual reconciliation, a process that is historically error-prone and time-consuming. These operational blind spots inevitably lead to data lag, leaving management without a real-time view of total assets and complicating critical tasks such as tax compliance and profit auditing.
To address these inefficiencies, this research documents the development and implementation of the Cloud-Based Inventory and Financial Management System. This integrated approach is supported by recent findings in the field; for instance, Ayochok and Perez (2024) highlighted that enhancing inventory systems is critical for resolving inefficiencies caused by manual procedures in local institutions. Furthermore, transitioning to automated platforms significantly improves decision quality. As noted by CloudCFO (2025), cloud automation reduces overhead costs and provides management with real-time visibility into cash flow, which is essential for long-term sustainability. Additionally, comparative studies establish that system-driven inventory tools significantly outperform manual tracking in terms of speed and error reduction (Conde et al., 2026).
Ultimately, the shift toward a Cloud-Based Inventory and Financial Management System is no longer a luxury but a necessity for competitive survival. This study seeks to validate a technological framework specifically tailored to the resource constraints of Cebu City’s SMEs. By bridging the gap between physical inventory and digital financial records, this project aims to establish a "Single Source of Truth" that empowers local business owners to move from reactive troubleshooting to proactive strategic planning.

Scope and Limitations
The digital transformation of Small and Medium Enterprises (SMEs) in the Philippines is often hindered by fragmented systems that isolate critical business data. This study documents the development and evaluation of Kodigo, a browser-based, online-first inventory and financial management application for multi-branch SME operations. The system was shaped around the operational needs of SMEs in Cebu City. To define the boundaries of the investigation and the technical deliverables, the scope is divided into the Scope of the Study, which outlines the research parameters and target users, and the Scope of the System, which details the implemented features and architecture.
















Scope of the Study
This study focuses on the design, implementation, and evaluation of a Cloud-Based Inventory and Financial Management System to address synchronization challenges between physical inventory and financial records in SMEs. In the implemented role structure, the Super Admin is limited to generating invite codes for Admin account creation, business owners function as Admin users, and operational staff use the system according to assigned permissions, including Cashier and Inventory roles.
a. Business owners, who function as Admin users and require store-level and multi-store visibility for strategic decision-making and operational oversight
b. Operational staff, particularly Cashiers and Inventory personnel, who use assigned permissions for daily work. Cashiers process sales and receipts. Inventory users maintain products and base-unit stock, record adjustments, and review inventory-oriented reports. Supplier purchasing and the dedicated restocking and supplier-management workflows are handled by Admin users.

The study focuses on the integration of POS transactions with a centralized inventory database to maintain a shared authoritative record across participating branches. It covers system development, framework validation, security evaluation, and user acceptability assessment. The implemented application includes descriptive analytics, product rankings, and sales-velocity views derived from recorded activity, but does not include AI-driven forecasting or predictive machine learning.



Scope of the System
The technical development of the Cloud-Based Inventory and Financial Management System includes the following core functionalities and architectural components:
Core Functionality: The system combines connected POS and inventory workflows with base-unit stock control, configured selling options, supplier and purchase-order management, sales and inventory reporting, and consignment tracking. For consigned goods, the agreed supplier value is recorded as payable when the goods are received; Admin users record payments or credits and review the resulting supplier balance.
Technical Stack: The implemented application uses React, TypeScript, and Vite for the frontend; Tailwind CSS, Zustand, and TanStack Query for interface, state, and data-query management; and Supabase PostgreSQL, Auth, Row Level Security, Storage, RPC, and Edge Functions for backend services. IndexedDB supports bounded browser caching and mutation queueing. jsPDF and write-excel-file support document and report outputs. QZ Tray provides an optional local connection for cash-drawer pulses through a selected printer. Vercel SPA routing configuration supports deployment.
Information Security: The framework includes Supabase authentication, invite-based onboarding, role-based access control, TOTP multi-factor authentication, Row Level Security, server-side validation, and protected functions. AAL2 assurance is used for protected operations when a factor is enrolled and required. These controls separate Super Admin invite-code governance, Admin operations, Cashier sales, and Inventory work within assigned store scope.



Limitations of the Study
Specific Focus: The study will only explore the implementation of the cloud-based framework for inventory and financial synchronization ; it does not cover external factors like supply chain logistics, fleet management, or raw material procurement.
Implementation Depth: The research evaluates the developed prototype/application within the selected SME context and supported store scope rather than claiming a full-scale national deployment.
Security Limitations: While the system addresses data protection, the study does not cover full-scale cybersecurity solutions or the specifics of advanced international security certifications.

Limitations of the System
The system provides descriptive analytics, rankings, sales velocity, and values derived from recorded transactions; it does not include Artificial Intelligence (AI) or Machine Learning (ML) forecasting. It is not certified accounting software and does not file taxes with the Bureau of Internal Revenue (BIR). The consignment module records the agreed value of received goods, Admin-entered supplier payments or credits, and outstanding balances; it is not a general accounts-payable system or general ledger. The application is online-first. It supports bounded browser caching and queueing for selected POS or inventory mutations, but not comprehensive offline reporting or automatic conflict resolution.

Software Developers: This project documents the integration of frontend application workflows with cloud database services for multi-store retail operations. CHAPTER 2
REVIEW OF RELATED LITERATURE
Recent studies emphasize the increasing role of digital technologies in transforming the operational practices of Small and Medium Enterprises (SMEs), particularly in inventory and financial management. Cloud computing has emerged as a significant technological innovation that allows SMEs to improve operational efficiency and enhance decision-making processes. Cloud-based financial systems allow organizations to access updated financial information from any location through internet-based platforms, enabling managers to monitor financial performance continuously and make faster and more accurate financial decisions (Nguyen & Hoang, 2025). Similarly, cloud-based accounting information systems improve financial reporting by enabling managers to observe transactions and operational activities in real time, allowing organizations to identify inconsistencies early and maintain more reliable financial records (Vo Van et al., 2026). Cloud accounting systems improve organizational coordination by allowing multiple departments to access shared financial data while automating financial transactions, which enhances the efficiency of financial management processes within SMEs (Septiawan et al., 2025). Cloud-based management information systems enhance organizational decision-making by providing centralized access to operational data and real-time analytical tools that allow managers to analyze business performance more effectively (Ashrafuzzaman, 2024). Real-time inventory monitoring systems allow businesses to track stock levels continuously, which reduces discrepancies and improves inventory control within SMEs (Daniel & Archie, 2024). Integrating predictive analytics with real-time inventory monitoring allows organizations to anticipate demand patterns and make better decisions regarding stock replenishment and inventory planning (Nasir & Martha, 2024).
Overall, the literature indicates that cloud-based inventory and financial management systems provide SMEs with centralized data management, automated financial processes, and real-time operational monitoring, which reduces inefficiencies associated with manual systems and enables managers to make faster and more informed decisions.

Challenges and Operational Bottlenecks Faced by Small and Medium-Sized Enterprises
Small and medium enterprises continue to encounter operational bottlenecks when inventory records and financial data are maintained through manual, disconnected processes. One recurring problem is inventory record inaccuracy. Shabani et al. (2021) explained that inventory record inaccuracy occurs when the quantity recorded in the inventory system does not match the quantity physically available. In practical SME operations, this mismatch can result from delayed stock encoding, manual stock counts, and branch-level recording inconsistencies, which contribute to stock discrepancies, stockout risks, and unreliable inventory information for day-to-day decisions. Manual financial reconciliation also creates reporting delays and workflow inefficiencies. Kosadi et al. (2021) found that when data from multiple transaction platforms are manually integrated into internal reports, the process tends to produce duplicated records, longer reporting cycles, and a greater chance of error. For SMEs that still reconcile sales, inventory movements, and financial records through spreadsheets or separate branch files, reconciliation becomes dependent on repeated checking, matching, and correction before reports can be finalized. This slows the release of financial information needed for operational and managerial decisions. Another bottleneck is the lack of real-time visibility across inventory and financial records. When stock updates and transaction records are encoded manually and reconciled only after transactions have accumulated, business owners and managers are left with delayed and sometimes inconsistent operational data. As noted by Kosadi et al. (2021), records duplication and non-automated reconciliation processes prolong processing time, while Shabani et al. (2021) showed that inaccurate inventory records can distort inventory and sales management performance. In multi-branch SME settings, these conditions can intensify reporting inefficiencies, create uncertainty in stock monitoring, and increase the amount of time spent resolving discrepancies rather than supporting routine operations.

System Frameworks and Information Security Measures of the Proposed System
The reviewed literature indicates that an effective cloud-based inventory and financial management system for SMEs should be grounded in a centralized and scalable system framework. Luo (2024) explained that, within a cloud computing environment, the construction mode of SME accounting informatization should be evaluated using factors such as cost, safety, stability, flexibility, expansibility, technical ability, and human resources. Applied to the proposed system, this supports a framework in which inventory, sales, and financial records are stored in a shared cloud environment so that branch-level transactions can be synchronized into a unified database structure. A centralized cloud framework is also relevant because the proposed system combines operational and financial data that must remain consistent across users and locations. Luo (2024) further noted that cloud computing-based models are evaluated against traditional approaches in terms of flexibility and scalability, which are important for systems that need to support multiple users, branch transactions, and continuous updates. This supports the design of a cloud-based platform in which inventory tracking, transaction recording, and financial reporting are integrated within one system environment rather than maintained in isolated records. Information security measures are equally critical in the design of the proposed system. Nagahawatta et al. (2024) identified cloud security standards, data privacy concerns, data security concerns, skilled personnel, technology readiness, legal compliance, and trust in cloud service providers as security-related factors that shape SME cloud computing decisions. These factors suggest that the proposed system should not only store data in the cloud but also apply security controls that protect confidentiality, integrity, and authorized access to financial and inventory records. SMEs also face cybersecurity vulnerabilities due to limited protective measures, constrained financial resources, and inadequate cybersecurity expertise, which makes the inclusion of practical and cost-conscious security controls important in cloud-based system design (Manzoor et al., 2024). Literature on cloud security also emphasizes the role of authentication and access control in securing cloud-based systems. Mohammad (2022) stated that access control protects confidentiality by ensuring that data are disclosed only to authorized users and protects integrity by limiting alteration of resources to legitimate users. In the context of the proposed system, this supports the use of user authentication mechanisms and role-based access control so that business owners, managers, and staff members are granted only the functions and records appropriate to their roles. For cloud-native and scalable systems, secure communication and authorization mechanisms must also be considered in the framework design. Rahaman et al. (2023) reported that role-based access control was the most efficient access control mechanism in the reviewed cloud-native studies and noted that secure communication should implement Mutual Transport Layer Security (mTLS) or JSON Web Token (JWT). These findings support the inclusion of authenticated user sessions, protected API communication, and role-based permissions in the proposed cloud-based inventory and financial management system to strengthen secure access and data protection. 
Acceptability Level of the Proposed Cloud-Based Inventory and Financial Management System
Research examining technology adoption among small and medium enterprises highlights several factors that influence the acceptability of cloud-based systems. Relative advantage, compatibility, complexity, organizational readiness, and top management support significantly influence SMEs’ intentions to adopt cloud accounting systems (Rashid et al., 2024). Organizational capabilities also play an important role in technology adoption. Digital culture, top management support, human resource capability, and adoption costs are among the most important predictors of digital technology adoption in SMEs (Faiz et al., 2024). Operational efficiency improvements are also important determinants of system acceptance. Data-driven financial management systems improve organizational decision-making by providing real-time financial insights that support budgeting, forecasting, and strategic planning (Ige et al., 2024). System reliability is another important factor influencing technology acceptance among SMEs. Cloud-based accounting systems enhance organizational performance by enabling continuous system availability, improved data integrity, and real-time access to financial information (Vo Van et al., 2026). Security considerations also influence adoption decisions. Security and privacy concerns remain critical factors influencing SME adoption of cloud computing technologies (Nagahawatta et al., 2024).





Summary of the Review of Related Literature
The literature reviewed in this chapter shows that manual inventory tracking and financial reconciliation create persistent operational bottlenecks for SMEs, particularly in the areas of inventory discrepancies, delayed reconciliation, limited real-time visibility, and inefficient reporting workflows. These issues affect the quality and timeliness of information available to business owners and managers, making routine and strategic decisions more difficult. The reviewed works also show that a cloud-based system framework is appropriate for integrating inventory, sales, and financial records within a centralized platform. A well-designed cloud framework supports synchronized data processing, shared access across authorized users, and a structure that can adapt to the operational requirements of growing SMEs. The literature further highlights that system design must be supported by appropriate security measures. Authentication controls, role-based access, secure data transmission, and broader data protection practices are essential in protecting sensitive operational and financial information stored in the cloud. Taken together, the reviewed literature supports the need for a cloud-based inventory and financial management system that can reduce the inefficiencies of manual processes while providing SMEs with a more integrated, secure, and decision-oriented platform.







CHAPTER 3
RESEARCH AND SYSTEM DEVELOPMENT METHODOLOGY
This chapter presents the methodology used to design, develop, and evaluate the proposed Cloud-Based Inventory and Financial Management System for small and medium enterprises (SMEs). It describes the study's method and research design, sampling approach, data gathering tools and techniques, procedures for data collection, project timeline, estimated cost, software development methodology, conceptual framework, software development tools, use-case diagram, and data analysis tools and techniques. The methodological choices were aligned with the study's three research questions so that existing operational problems could be examined, translated into system requirements, and evaluated through a functional prototype.

Research Framework and Design
This study adopts a mixed-method approach arranged in an exploratory sequential manner. The qualitative component is used first to understand the operational realities of SMEs that still rely on manual or partially digitized inventory tracking and financial reconciliation. Insights from this phase inform the system requirements, workflow logic, and information security considerations of the proposed cloud-based system. The quantitative component is then used to describe baseline operational conditions and to assess the post-implementation acceptability of the prototype. Mixed-method inquiry is appropriate for applied information systems research because it enables researchers to combine narrative explanations of user experience with numerical indicators of operational conditions and system evaluation (Creswell & Plano Clark, 2017; Fetters et al., 2013; Morgan, 2013). 
Research Question 1 is addressed through a mixed-method strand. Qualitative interview data are used to identify operational bottlenecks, while quantitative baseline questionnaire data are used to describe the frequency and extent of current problems such as inventory discrepancies, delayed reconciliation, and data inconsistency. Research Question 2 is addressed through a quantitative strand. Structured technical validation ratings are used to determine the appropriateness of the proposed system framework and information security measures for the cloud-based solution. Research Question 3 is addressed through a quantitative strand. Survey responses gathered after prototype interaction are used to determine the level of acceptability of the system in terms of cost-effectiveness, system reliability, operational efficiency, and security.
	Three complementary research designs support the method adopted in this study. First, a descriptive design is used for Research Question 1. This design is appropriate because the study must document current inventory and financial management practices and describe the operational consequences of manual or fragmented processes in real SME settings. Descriptive inquiry is suitable when the goal is to present the characteristics of an existing condition as experienced by participants and observed in practice. Second, a developmental-evaluative design is used for Research Question 2. This design is appropriate for information technology studies that move from problem diagnosis to system planning, prototyping, refinement, and quantitative technical validation. In the present study, the developmental-evaluative design translates operational needs into system modules, user roles, data flows, and security safeguards, then uses structured validation ratings to determine whether the proposed framework and information security measures are appropriate for the intended SME environment. Prior research also indicates that meaningful user involvement contributes to higher system relevance and implementation success (Bano et al., 2015). Third, an evaluative design is used for Research Question 3. After the prototype is developed and exposed to intended users, an evaluative design makes it possible to assess whether the system is acceptable and useful in the target setting. This is consistent with information systems acceptance literature, which emphasizes the importance of user perceptions, system characteristics, and performance benefits in determining likely adoption and continued use 
Overall, the selected framework is appropriate because it connects diagnosis, development, and evaluation within one coherent study. The design allows the researchers to begin with the actual problems faced by SMEs, convert those problems into technical requirements, and then determine whether the resulting system is acceptable for practical use.

Sampling and Techniques
This study uses non-probability purposive sampling, specifically criterion-based purposive sampling with elements of maximum variation sampling. The sampling technique is appropriate because the study requires participants who have direct and relevant experience in inventory monitoring, sales recording, financial reconciliation, and system use within SME operations, as well as qualified technical evaluators capable of assessing the proposed system framework and information security measures. Criterion-based purposive sampling ensures that all participants possess the characteristics necessary to answer the research questions, while maximum variation strengthens the study by including participants from the partner enterprise and from similar SMEs with comparable operational conditions (Palinkas et al., 2015). The main participant criteria are as follows: (1) for the interview phase, the participant is a business owner or manager of the partner SME or of a comparable SME; (2) for the survey phase, the participant is a business owner, manager, or operations staff member involved in daily sales, inventory-related work, or financial reconciliation; and (3) for the technical validation phase, the participant is a qualified IT instructor, system developer, database specialist, or information systems practitioner capable of evaluating the appropriateness of the proposed system framework and security measures. Including participants from comparable SMEs broadens the empirical base of the study and reduces the risk of drawing conclusions from a single organizational context only. A case-oriented approach supported by comparable settings is appropriate when the study seeks to understand patterns across real-world environments rather than isolate variables in a laboratory setting (Crowe et al., 2011). The adequacy of the qualitative interview sample is supported by the information power of the selected respondents because interview participants are deliberately chosen for their managerial and decision-making relevance (Malterud et al., 2016). For the quantitative components, all identified operational respondents will be included in the baseline and acceptability surveys, while multiple qualified technical evaluators will be engaged to provide structured validation ratings for Research Question 2. Data Gathering Tools and Techniques
Data gathering in this study is organized according to the method assigned to each research question. Separate instruments were prepared for qualitative inquiry, baseline quantitative profiling, and post-implementation system evaluation. To maintain methodological clarity, the tools refer to the instruments used to collect information, while the techniques refer to the procedures through which those instruments are administered.

Data Gathering Tools
Semi-Structured Interview Guide. This instrument will be used with business owners/managers only to obtain detailed descriptions of current workflows, recurring stock discrepancies, reconciliation delays, cost implications, and decision-making challenges. Semi-structured interviews are appropriate because they provide consistent topics while leaving room for participants to elaborate on managerial realities and contextual issues (Kallio et al., 2016). The tool supports the qualitative component of Research Question 1.
Baseline Operational Questionnaire. This structured questionnaire will be administered before prototype deployment to obtain quantitative information about current inventory tracking methods, the occurrence of discrepancies, reconciliation time, and perceived operational delays. The tool supports the quantitative component of Research Question 1 by describing the baseline condition of participating SMEs in a form that can be summarized statistically and visually.
System Acceptability Survey. This post-implementation survey will be accomplished by intended users after interacting with the prototype. The instrument measures the proposed system in terms of cost-effectiveness, system reliability, operational efficiency, and security. These dimensions are aligned with information systems success and software quality literature, particularly system quality, net benefits, reliability, and security considerations (International Organization for Standardization & International Electrotechnical Commission, 2023). This tool directly supports Research Question 3.
Technical Validation Survey. This structured survey will be accomplished by qualified technical evaluators to examine whether the prototype’s framework and security controls are appropriate for a cloud-based SME environment. The instrument focuses on authentication, role-based access control, data integrity, centralized synchronization, and secure data handling, and it supports Research Question 2 through quantitative validation ratings.

Data Gathering Techniques
Semi-Structured Interviewing. The researchers will conduct guided interviews with business owners/managers only. This technique is suited to eliciting detailed explanations of how manual or fragmented processes affect managerial oversight, operational control, reconciliation practices, and business decision-making.
Survey Administration. Structured questionnaires will be administered before and after implementation. Pre-implementation survey administration captures baseline operational indicators, while post-implementation survey administration captures user judgments about the prototype.
Guided Prototype Evaluation. After development, selected users will perform common tasks such as logging in, processing sales, updating inventory records, and viewing reports. This technique ensures that respondents evaluate the system after direct interaction rather than through abstract description alone.
Technical Validation Survey Administration. Qualified technical evaluators will review the prototype using the structured validation survey to assess the appropriateness of the system framework and information security measures.
The combination of interviews, surveys, guided system use, and technical validation is appropriate because it produces rich qualitative descriptions and measurable quantitative evidence. In this study, the interviews with business owners/managers provide managerial and decision-oriented qualitative insights, while the baseline operational questionnaire, technical validation survey, and system acceptability survey provide quantitative evidence regarding baseline conditions, framework appropriateness, and system acceptability. This combination reflects the integrative logic of mixed-method studies in which qualitative data explain the nature of a problem and quantitative data support description and evaluation (Creswell & Plano Clark, 2017; Fetters et al., 2013).












Procedures for Data Collection
The data collection procedure was carried out in three major phases to ensure the systematic gathering of both qualitative and quantitative data needed to address the study objectives. These phases included the pre-implementation phase, the system development and controlled deployment phase, and the post-implementation phase. This step-by-step procedure enabled the researchers to first examine the existing operational practices of the selected SME partner organization, then develop and deploy the proposed system in a controlled setting, and finally assess its acceptability and potential contribution to improving decision-making efficiency.
 • Pre-Implementation Phase
• Coordinate with the selected SME partner organization in Cebu City and secure permission to conduct the study.
• Conduct preliminary interviews with the business owner/manager of the partner SME and the owners/managers of selected comparable SMEs to identify key operational bottlenecks, reconciliation issues, and decision-making challenges related to inventory monitoring, sales recording, and financial reconciliation.
• Administer the pre-implementation questionnaire to gather baseline quantitative data on current tracking methods, reconciliation practices, and operational delays.
• Consolidate and review the qualitative and quantitative baseline results to identify operational bottlenecks and translate them into system requirements.
• System Development and Controlled Deployment
• Develop the proposed Cloud-Based Inventory and Financial Management System using the Agile development methodology.
• Deploy the functional prototype in a controlled testing environment within the partner organization.
• Allow selected users to perform representative tasks such as recording sales transactions, updating inventory records, and viewing operational reports.
• Post-Implementation Phase
• Administer the System Acceptability Survey after the evaluation period to measure cost-effectiveness, system reliability, operational efficiency, and security.
• Administer the Technical Validation Survey to qualified technical evaluators to obtain quantitative ratings on the appropriateness of the system framework and information security measures.
• Compile and analyze the collected data to determine the extent to which the proposed system can improve decision-making efficiency in the SME environment.

Software Development Methodology

Figure 3.1. Agile Development Cycle Used in the Study
This study strictly utilizes the Agile Scrum software development methodology to engineer the cloud-based platform. Scrum is an iterative and incremental framework that is highly effective for managing complex software development where user requirements may evolve as functional prototypes are exposed to the target environment. Unlike the linear Waterfall model, or the purely continuous flow of Kanban, Scrum provides a highly structured cadence through fixed-length development cycles known as Sprints. Agile Scrum is appropriate because the proposed system must be refined through repeated user feedback and because SME requirements may become clearer as the prototype evolves. Studies on software development show that Agile Scrum supports responsiveness, collaboration, and continuous improvement, particularly in projects where user needs shape product features during development (Beck et al., 2001; Mishra & Alzoubi, 2023; Strode et al., 2022).
Plan. The researchers will consolidate findings from the baseline study and identify system goals, functional requirements, user roles, and scope boundaries.
Design. The researchers will prepare the database structure, interface layout, process flow, and security controls such as authentication and access separation.
Develop. The prototype modules for point-of-sale processing, inventory management, dashboard monitoring, and reporting will be coded incrementally so that each component can be reviewed and improved.
Test. Each iteration will undergo functional testing to verify the correctness of transaction recording, inventory updates, and report generation and to identify errors that require correction.
Deploy. A working prototype will be placed in a controlled environment for user interaction and operational evaluation.
Review. User feedback and validator comments will be gathered and used to refine the prototype before final evaluation.
Launch. The finalized version will serve as the completed system output of the study and the basis for the acceptability assessment.

Data Analysis Tools and Techniques
The collected data will be analyzed using qualitative and quantitative procedures consistent with the mixed-method structure of the study. The qualitative component will be analyzed through thematic analysis, while the quantitative component will be processed and visualized using Power BI supported by descriptive statistics. The use of both approaches enables the researchers to interpret lived operational experiences and measurable evaluation results in a complementary manner.
Interview responses will be transcribed, organized, and analyzed using thematic analysis. Following Braun and Clarke (2006) and the trustworthiness guidance of Nowell et al. (2017), the researchers will (1) familiarize themselves with the data, (2) generate initial codes, (3) search for candidate themes, (4) review and refine the themes, (5) define and name the themes, and (6) interpret the findings in relation to the research questions. This approach is appropriate because it allows recurring patterns in workflow problems and decision-making challenges to emerge directly from participant accounts rather than being imposed in advance. Thematic analysis will be used for the qualitative component of Research Question 1.
The quantitative responses from the baseline operational questionnaire, the technical validation survey, and the system acceptability survey will be encoded and imported into Power BI. Power BI will be used to organize the dataset, create calculated measures, and present the results through tables, indicator cards, bar charts, and other dashboard visuals. This tool is appropriate because business intelligence platforms improve the clarity of numerical findings and help transform survey results into decision-oriented visual summaries (Hurbean et al., 2023; Raihanto et al., 2023).
For the quantitative component of Research Question 1, Power BI will be used to present baseline descriptive results such as frequency and percentage distributions of current inventory tracking methods, occurrence of discrepancies, and time required for reconciliation. For Research Question 2, Power BI will be used to compute and visualize the mean and sample standard deviation of the technical validation ratings for the proposed system framework and information security measures. For Research Question 3, Power BI will be used to compute and visualize the mean and sample standard deviation of the acceptability ratings under the criteria of cost-effectiveness, system reliability, operational efficiency, and security.

CHAPTER 4
RESULTS AND DISCUSSIONS
This chapter presents, analyzes, and interprets the findings for Research Question 1, which sought to identify the key challenges and operational bottlenecks faced by small and medium enterprises in manual inventory tracking and error-prone financial reconciliation, particularly in terms of inaccurate inventory tracking, slow decision-making, high operational cost, and data inconsistency. The discussion integrates baseline pre-survey results from operational respondents and thematic analysis of interview transcripts. Taken together, the findings show that the central operational problem is not merely the use of manual tools, but the absence of one timely, trusted, and shared source of truth for stock movement, sales, pricing, and related financial records.

Response Rate
In the study's assessment of response rates, all intended operational respondents completed the pre-survey, and all identified interview transcripts for the qualitative strand were included in the thematic analysis. Specifically, for the quantitative portion, 18 out of 18 target participants completed the pre-survey, resulting in a 100.0% response rate. Similarly, the qualitative portion saw full participation, with 5 out of 5 target interview transcripts analyzed, also yielding a 100.0% response rate. This total participation provided full coverage of the available baseline quantitative data and the cross-case qualitative accounts used to answer Research Question 1.



Demographics
The pre-survey respondents represented both decision-making and day-to-day operational roles. Operations staff formed the largest group, followed by owners or managers, cashiers or sales encoders, and stock custodians. The distribution of years in role also indicates that the dataset reflects both relatively new and experienced personnel, allowing the chapter to capture operational bottlenecks from multiple perspectives. In addition, the operational profile already suggests fragmented workflows, since most respondents relied on a combination of two or more methods rather than a single integrated tool.

The pre-survey results show that manual and fragmented processes remain deeply embedded in current SME operations. Fragmented recordkeeping creates no single source of truth
The first theme shows that records are distributed across notebooks, spreadsheets, chat messages, POS summaries, receipts, and memory. In Interviewee 1’s account, inventory decisions depended largely on checking which items looked low and then contacting another person to verify prices. Similar fragmentation appeared in the grocery, hardware, and bakery cases, where branch transfers, release slips, production logs, or chat updates could exist separately from the main stock file. This directly reflects data inconsistency, because each tool contains only a partial version of the operational reality.

Delayed updates make the accurate picture arrive too late
The second theme highlights the timing problem. Interviewee 3 explained that the real situation often becomes visible too late, while Interviewee 5 emphasized that by the time all records are complete, the opportunity to adjust production, labor, or restocking may already be gone. This theme explains why survey respondents frequently reported delayed purchasing, report submission, and discrepancy investigation. The issue is therefore not merely whether the record becomes accurate eventually, but whether it becomes accurate in time to guide action.







Inaccurate records disrupt service, fulfillment, and restocking
The third theme connects data problems to visible operational outcomes. Interviewees described customers being told that stock was available when the shelf count said otherwise, partial releases for promised orders, and more cautious restocking because the numbers could not be trusted. In the hardware case, an incomplete record reduced the ability to commit materials to a contractor. In the bakery case, delayed information led to conservative production decisions and missed sales opportunities. These accounts show that inaccurate tracking weakens both service reliability and inventory responsiveness.

Manual reconciliation shifts effort from operations to error correction
The fourth theme clarifies why operational cost is not limited to direct cash loss. Interviewees repeatedly described overtime, repeated checking, recounting, follow-up calls, document chasing, and after-hours correction. Survey results mirror this pattern, since every respondent reported spending at least 30 minutes per reconciliation cycle and all respondents reported incurring extra costs due to errors at least once or twice in a typical month. The cost is therefore both monetary and organizational, because staff attention is diverted from serving customers and planning the business.

When records conflict, trust shifts to physical proof
The fifth theme reveals a trust deficit in the record system itself. When numbers do not match, respondents do not immediately trust the spreadsheet, notebook, or report; they return to physical counts, signed slips, delivery receipts, and screenshots. This explains why discrepancy tracing often takes 30 minutes or more. It also shows that the existing record structure does not yet function as a decision-ready source of truth. Instead, managers must repeatedly re-validate the record against physical evidence before acting on it.

Overall, the findings answer Research Question 1 by showing that the most critical operational bottleneck is the absence of a unified record that can support sales processing, stock visibility, and financial reconciliation. Inaccurate inventory tracking emerges when updates are delayed or scattered across tools. Slow decision-making occurs when staff must verify records before acting. High operational cost arises from rework, overtime, emergency replenishment, wasted effort, and missed opportunities. Data inconsistency persists because different documents or systems capture only fragments of the same event. These baseline findings support the system's focus on connected, server-validated records and bounded queueing for selected operations. They describe pre-existing conditions and do not, by themselves, measure post-implementation performance reductions.

Summary of Findings
The quantitative and qualitative findings converge on the same conclusion. Current SME practices are characterized by fragmented recordkeeping, delayed updating, repeated reconciliation, and weak confidence in the records used for decisions. Most respondents relied on multiple tools, experienced recurring discrepancies, and reported that the current workflow increased workload and delayed operational action. The interview data further showed that these problems are felt directly through stock uncertainty, slower replenishment, customer service disruption, and greater dependence on physical verification.
More specifically, the chapter found that inaccurate inventory tracking is sustained by delayed postings, missed entries, and multiple non-synchronized records. Slow decision-making arises because usable information is often available only after additional checking. High operational cost results from overtime, emergency buying, repeated counting, and missed sales. Data inconsistency persists because different sources of information do not align at the same time or with the same level of detail. These findings establish that the problem addressed in this study is both operational and informational: without a shared, timely, and trusted record, SMEs are forced to operate reactively instead of making confident, evidence-based decisions.
